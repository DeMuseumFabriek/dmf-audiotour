Origin and Construction

You are standing in front of a Los Hoes (Loose House). This type of farmhouse originated around the year 1100.

Often, several of these farmhouses were situated close together. The residents helped each other when needed; this was called "noaberschap" (neighborhood) in Twente.

In the past, people built with materials found nearby.

Such a farmhouse consisted of a frame of wooden beams, called anchor trusses.

The walls were made of wickerwork of twigs, which they sealed with a mixture of manure, loam, and straw.

The facade was made of wooden planks and the roof of thatch.

Upstairs, there was space to store grain, and the open fire allowed food to be cooked and meat to be smoked in the attic.

In Twente, the Achterhoek region, South Drenthe, and the neighboring part of Germany, farmhouses were often built this way.

The Los Hoes in the museum is more modern. It has stone walls, roof tiles, and a chimney.

Over the centuries, living there became more comfortable, and eventually a real chimney was added.

If you look closely, you'll see that this Los Hoes farm isn't entirely complete.

The left wing is missing because there wasn't enough space in the museum.

That's where the cows were usually kept for manure and milk.

The frame of these farms consisted of anchor trusses and wooden beams.

The walls were made of wickerwork made of twigs and clay. The roof rested on the anchor trusses, and the roofing was straw.
