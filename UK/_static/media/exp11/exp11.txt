You are now in the engine cellar. The main attraction here is, of course, the steam engine Agneta. You can read more information about it. Another highlight is the large white marble switchboard on the back wall.
For a long time, all the machines in factories were powered by steam engines. One steam engine could drive an entire factory through various shafts and other drive belts. Around 1920, small, reliable electric motors were introduced to the market. These were placed next to the textile machines, and with a small drive belt, the machines could operate. With the push of a button, the electric motors could be switched on and off. Initially, the electricity was generated by the “old” steam engines, as they powered dynamos or generators. These dynamos then supplied the electricity that powered the electric motors.
In the corner, with the square plate above it, you can see a generator like that.
Even more efficient was power generation through a power plant. The electricity came into the factory via high-voltage cables. In the factory, there were transformers that converted the electricity to make it suitable for the electric motors. The white switchboard controlled the power supply in the factory. It was, in a way, a distribution station for the electric motors.
This switchboard comes from Spinning Mill Deutschland in Gronau. Several textile manufacturers from Twente were also owners of factories just across the border in Germany. In the Netherlands, wages were lower than in Germany, and by building factories there, they could produce for the German market without import duties. Every day, hundreds of workers crossed the border at Glanerbrug to work in Germany.
Spinning Mill Deutschland was founded in 1896 by Willem Jordaan and had a steam engine with 800 horsepower to drive all the spinning machines. In 1921, capital was raised to power all the machines individually with electricity. At that time, this Siemens-Schuckert switchboard was installed.
