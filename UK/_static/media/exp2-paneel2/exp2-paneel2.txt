Something remarkable happened in Great Britain around the mid-18th century: a true revolution in industry! In 1764, James Watt invented the steam engine, leading to the design of new machines and the construction of factories.  
In the early 19th century, wealthy entrepreneurs brought these kinds of machines to Twente, enabling the local textile industry to expand significantly. Along with the machines came British experts and entrepreneurs. One of these entrepreneurs was Job Dixon from Manchester, who also designed machines. Job was married to Mary Whitacker. Their portraits are slightly to the right of the centre, numbers 29 and 36.  
Job and Mary travelled with their children via Paris, Alsace, and Ghent to the Netherlands. King Willem Ⅰ had a plan to stimulate the textile industry, particularly in Twente. By then, there was already a steam-powered spinning mill in Almelo, and another was established in Enschede. In 1833, Job Dixon became the director of this "Great Steam" mill. Job had extensive knowledge of machines but less experience in business management, which led to conflicts. Eventually, he was dismissed in 1839. However, both Job and his son John remained important figures in the Dutch industry.  
Above Job Dixon’s portrait, on the left, hangs the portrait of John Tattersall (number 44), another Brit who settled in Enschede. Around 1870, he represented several British machine manufacturers and frequently visited Twente, where the textile industry had by then become highly successful. He visited all the major textile factories in the area. It soon became clear that John had a deep understanding of machine technology, and he devised improvements for the existing machines. His ideas were highly innovative and much appreciated by the manufacturers. As a result, he decided to establish his own machine factory in Enschede with his brother. They not only produced textile machines but also machinery for ships and the oil and gas industries.  
John Tattersall passed away in 1935. His factory continued to operate until 1972, when it was closed, like much of the Twente textile industry during that period. The site of the factory later became the Tattersall residential area.
