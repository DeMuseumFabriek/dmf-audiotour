The animal kingdom, of which human beings are also a part, consists of vertebrates and invertebrates. We have a backbone, so we are vertebrates. Life originally began in the sea. When the first animals moved onto land, they developed bones. These bones have hardly changed since then. From mouse to mammoth, nearly every animal has about the same number of ribs, fingers, toes, and vertebrae. A giraffe has the same number of vertebrae in its neck as a mole! When vertebrates are born, they have about 300 bones. Over the course of their lives, some of these bones fuse together. By the time a human being is very old and passes away, he or she will have just over 200 bones left from the original 270 bones.  
Animals live in all sorts of different environments, and that’s why evolution has led to differences in their skeletons. Birds, for example, have smaller skeletons but 14 neck vertebrae. That's twice as many as a giraffe! Snakes have a lot of vertebrae. Did you know that snakes once had legs, just like whales? In some snakes and whales, the remnants of those legs are still present in their bodies, as small bones. But not in the snake that is displayed here in the exhibition.  
Usually, the bones that an animal has are enough to make small adaptations. For example, it seems as if a mole has six fingers on each paw, but that sixth ‘finger’ is actually an extra small bone.  
Animals with heavy heads, large antlers, or large horns all have a bump on their backs. This bump grows because the bones in their necks lengthen. These bones have spiny projections, to which strong muscles are attached. These muscles extend towards the head. This is how the muscles act like a lever, allowing the animal to lift its heavy head. Take a look at the skeletons of the woolly mammoth, the giant deer, and the steppe bison in this hall.
