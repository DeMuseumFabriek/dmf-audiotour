Twente has long been known as a textile region with an industrial past, but it has also been an agricultural area for centuries. Since the Middle Ages, a landscape of irregular fields and meadows surrounded by shrubs and hedgerows has gradually developed. This is known as a *coulisse* landscape.  
Between the farmland, farmers lived in a *los hoes*, a typical Twente farmhouse where people and animals shared the same open space without partitions. Look at paintings 79 and 80, to the left of the centre, and painting 98, all the way to the left.  
Most of the land originally belonged to the nobility, but by around 1750, small farmers increasingly became owners of the land they worked. Unused land was cultivated and divided. In Twente, much of this land ended up in the hands of factory owners, who often used it for forestry, logging, and hunting.  
The textile factories required good infrastructure to transport goods. Therefore, during the mid-19th century, significant investments were made in building canals and railways.  
For a long time, the peatlands remained untouched. However, with better transport options, the peat was excavated. Peat, a type of fuel, was used to power the factories.  
These industrial developments transformed the Twente landscape. Fortunately, many beautiful and picturesque areas remained, such as the Aamsveen near Enschede (painting 100).  
By the late 19th century, people began to take an interest in nature conservation, recognising the importance of landscapes. Around this time, factory owners also started investing in natural areas. They built country houses outside the city and hired renowned landscape architects to design the surrounding parks and gardens. In doing so, they altered the Twente landscape once again.  
Artists captured these new developments in their paintings. Wealthy individuals appreciated having a Twente landscape displayed above their sofas. On this wall, you can see several examples of such works.
