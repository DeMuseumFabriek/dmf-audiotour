Twente was home to a vast textile industry in the 19th and 20th centuries. But did you know that spinning and weaving were already taking place in this region during prehistoric times? In front of you, you can see a farm from the first century AD.  
We know that sheep were kept, and flax was grown during that time. Wool from sheep and fibres from the inside of the flax stem can be used to make thread. And with that thread, you can weave. In the small field next to the farm, barley or wheat is probably being cultivated, but it could also be a flax field.  
What’s missing from this model is a loom. Back then, looms were not horizontal like they are now but vertical. The threads were tied to a stick at the top. At the bottom, weaving weights were attached to keep the threads taut. Other threads were then woven through these vertical threads.  
You can see weaving weights in the top drawer on the far right. In the drawer below, you’ll find round stones with a hole in the middle. These are spindle whorls. A stick was inserted through the hole to create a kind of spinning top. With one hand, people spun the top, while with the other, they wound a small amount of wool or flax onto the stick bit by bit. By twisting their fingers in a spinning motion, they created thread. This thread could then be used for weaving and knitting.  
The spindle whorls and weaving weights are from roughly the same time as the prehistoric farm as shown in the model.
