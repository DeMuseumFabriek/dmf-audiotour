Joseph-Marie Jacquard inherited a workshop with two looms. These looms could weave patterns into fabric. A shuttle with weft yarn passes evenly through the warp threads, back and forth. However, if you skip certain threads, for instance by raising specific warp threads, a pattern is created.
At that time, people would stand on a small platform on top of the loom. The weaver would instruct them on which warp threads to pull up, for example, 5, 8, 15, 24, 7, and so on. This was an exhausting and labour-intensive way to weave patterns.
In 1703, the German mathematician and philosopher Leibniz demonstrated that all information could be written in zeros and ones, the binary system that computers still use today. Jacquard knew of this theory, devised a punch card, and worked on his looms until he developed a functioning system where the punch card ran through a type of machine. The punch card consisted of holes and non-holes. A hole could be seen as a 1 and a non-hole as a 0. A rod that raised a warp thread could pass through a hole, but not through a non-hole. The punch card thus served as an instruction for the loom to weave a pattern. This was the world’s first application of digital technology.
This invention meant that the people over the loom were no longer needed. They were the first workers to lose their jobs due to computer automation. Out of anger, they destroyed Jacquard's factory.
However, Jacquard received widespread recognition for his invention, which was soon adopted in other factories. He was awarded a medal by Napoleon and given a pension. The Jacquard looms in the Museumfabriek are much more modern, dating from the 1970s. But the punch card system is still clearly visible.
