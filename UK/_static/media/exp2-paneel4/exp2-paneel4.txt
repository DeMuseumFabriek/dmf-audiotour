On the right-hand side of the wall, in the middle at number 8, hangs a painting of the Ten Cate Hoedemaker family. It was painted in 1825. You can see father Hendrik, mother Henriëtte, and their four children. Standing in the doorway is the painter of this portrait: A.W. Floh. Later, five more children were born into the family. Sadly, both parents died young, as did their son Jan and daughter Maria. However, some of the other children were more fortunate. A few married and had children of their own. Daughter Geertruida became an artist, and son Herman became a minister. Other sons became manufacturers, sailors, or craftsmen. The youngest son became a doctor.  
The same could be said for the group of boys depicted in painting number 28, slightly to the left. It was painted in 1855. Four of the six boys are sons of factory owners Stroink, Jordaan, and Blijdenstein. They are playing behind the church on the Oude Markt in Enschede. It appears they have the day off from school. For these boys, a good education was very important, as they may have wanted to follow in their fathers’ footsteps. Indeed, some of them went on to become factory owners themselves.  
However, education was not accessible to everyone at that time. Many children from poor families accompanied their parents to the factory in the morning to work, often under very harsh conditions. Child labour was entirely common practice back then.  
Around the mid-19th century, this began to change. In 1866, the Factory School was established, an initiative by several factory owners. Lessons were held before and after working hours, and a diploma from this school was required to work in one of the factories. This approach was a phenomenon in the Netherlands. It marked the beginning of the ‘Child Labour Law of Van Houten,’ which banned child labour in factories in 1874. However, enforcement of the law was minimal. It was only with the Compulsory Education Act of 1900 that child labour was definitively abolished.
