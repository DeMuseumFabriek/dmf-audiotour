If you look closely at the paintings on this wall, you may notice that some of them look alike.  
For instance, take the portrait of factory owner Herman van Lochem. There are three versions of it here, on numbers 1, 14, and 77. All three are copies of the original, which was lost in the great city fire of 1750.  
Also striking are the two paintings of the great city fire of 1862, displayed on the right at numbers 22 and 27. The two artists stood on the same spot in the Langestraat to capture the devastation. The fire started in the Kalanderstraat during a warm and dry spring. The streets were still decorated for the visit of King Willem Ⅲ to Enschede the week before. These decorations, along with the wooden houses, quickly caught fire. Of Enschede's 4,400 residents at the time, nearly 85% were left homeless!  
To the right, at numbers 20 and 21, are two small "hair paintings" from 1825. These are paintings incorporating hair from someone who has passed away, serving as a keepsake. This practice was especially popular in the 19th century. The hair was often finely trimmed or ground, mixed with glue, and applied to paper with a brush. Longer strands of hair were used to form the basis for trees and branches.  
At the top right and left are two small paintings of volcanoes (numbers 23 and 75). They were created in 1850 and 1906. Both depict eruptions of Mount Vesuvius in Italy and were likely souvenirs from a trip to Italy. It used to be common for young men from wealthy families to embark on a journey across Europe. Italy was a frequent destination, forming part of their education and cultural studies. Some sons of factory owners also undertook such a "Grand Tour."
