In front of you are two altar stones. These are copies; the originals from the year 230 are displayed in a museum near Hadrian’s Wall, near the border between present-day England and Scotland. These copies are also quite old, as they were made more than 100 years ago. The Latin inscription states that by erecting these altar stones, a vow has been fulfilled. The text is addressed to the god Mars Thingsus, two Germanic goddesses, and the divine spirit of Emperor Augustus. It states that the Germanic members of the Tuihanti tribe have voluntarily and justly fulfilled their promise.
The story goes that two members of the Tuihanti tribe were part of a Frisian military unit fighting as mercenaries for the Romans. The two soldiers were given a dangerous task. At a temple dedicated to the god Mars, they prayed and made a vow: "If I survive, I will set up an altar." This mission was probably in Scotland, at the time the land of the Picts. They survived the dangerous mission and erected these altar stones in honour of Mars.
Tacitus, a Roman historian, wrote in the year 98 AD that around the year 12, there was a battle between the Romans and the Germanic Marsi tribe. One of the tribes that came to the aid of the Marsi was the Tubantes, he wrote.
Tacitus’s account and these altar stones are the oldest references to the Tuihanti or Tubantes tribe. The name Tuihanti evolved through Tuente (11th century) to the word "Twente". From an archaeological perspective, Twente has been reasonably well studied, but unfortunately, no evidence has yet been found that a large Germanic tribe, like the Tubantes, lived here during Roman times.
