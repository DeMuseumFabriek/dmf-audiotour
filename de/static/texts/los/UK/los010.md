###### Working in the weaving room

Because natural fibers are stronger when slightly damp, the loom was often kept in a closed and unheated room.

The farmer worked here in winter from morning to evening, by oil lamplight.

The warp threads were sometimes strengthened with buckwheat or rye porridge, so that they did not break.

Inhaling flying fibers, oil fumes and porridge vapor often caused him to have respiratory problems.

He also often had pain in muscles and joints due to the same movements.

A folk rhyme went:

“There sat a weaver on his loom, gray with hunger and blue with cold.”

Another saying:

“A weaver doesn't eat his fill, but he doesn't get wet either.”

Did you know that the expression “warp and woof” was originally positive?

In the 18th and 19th centuries it was used for harmony and connection, just like in weaving.

Later it changed to a negative meaning, because it mainly came to mean repetition or something that happens often.
