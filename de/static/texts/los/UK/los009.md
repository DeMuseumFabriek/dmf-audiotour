###### Loom

Farmers were often self-sufficient.

They made clothes and blankets from flax and wool.

In the winter, when there was little work on the land, spinning and weaving took place.

Spinning was usually the work of women and children, weaving was often the work of the farmer himself.

Almost every farm had a weaving room with a loom.

Around the 10th and 11th centuries, the horizontal loom was introduced, replacing the vertical loom.

In the 13th century, pedals were introduced, with which the combs could be moved.

The loom here in Los Hoes dates from around 1650.

It has 2 pedals, with which the farmer could pull up half of the warp threads each time.

This created space between the threads to throw the spool of yarn through or, in Twente, throw it.

The weaving tray pressed the thread against the fabric and then the other half of the threads were lifted.

The cloth on the loom is colored linen.

That actually only happened later.

Linen naturally has a sandy or grayish-white color.

It was later bleached to make it whiter.

In 1728, Enschede received the exclusive right from the States of Overijssel to make bombazin (a mixture of linen and cotton).  

The cloth was about one cubit wide (approx. 69 cm), the distance between the elbows.

If it were wider, the throwing would be much harder.

A farmer could weave approximately two meters per day.

People wove for their own use, but if more was produced, it could be sold to a peddler.

They brought it to other villages or towns, so that the farmer could buy something extra that he could not make himself.
